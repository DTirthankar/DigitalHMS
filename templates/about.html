About the research project

An overview of the project background, objectives, team, tools, and the academic context underpinning this machine learning study.

Overview

Project background

Chronic and infectious diseases collectively account for a substantial portion of global morbidity and mortality. Early and accurate diagnosis remains a clinical and logistical challenge, particularly in settings with limited access to specialist care or diagnostic infrastructure. This project investigates the application of supervised machine learning classifiers to automate symptom-based disease identification.

Using a structured dataset of 4,920 patient records spanning 41 disease categories and 132 binary symptom features, the study evaluates the comparative performance of five widely-used classifiers: Random Forest, Decision Tree, Naïve Bayes, K-Nearest Neighbours, and Support Vector Machine. The goal is to determine which approach offers the most reliable and generalisable detection performance under consistent experimental conditions.

This work is intended as a foundational study demonstrating the feasibility of symptom-driven disease classification as a preliminary screening tool. It does not constitute a clinical decision-support system and is presented in an academic context only.

Aims

Research objectives

01
To preprocess and encode a real-world symptom-disease dataset for use in multi-class supervised classification tasks.
02
To train and evaluate five standard machine learning classifiers across identical experimental conditions, ensuring reproducibility of results.
03
To identify the most accurate classifier for this domain through accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and confusion matrix analysis.
04
To analyse feature importance to determine which symptoms carry the greatest discriminative value across disease classes.
05
To provide a transparent methodology suitable for extension, peer review, and replication in related healthcare ML research.

Scope

Limitations & future work

This study is subject to several limitations that should be considered when interpreting results or extending this work to applied settings.

Synthetic balance: the dataset contains exactly 120 records per class, which does not reflect real-world disease prevalence distributions. Classifier performance on imbalanced clinical data may differ significantly.
Binary symptom encoding: symptoms are represented as present or absent, discarding symptom severity, duration, and co-occurrence dynamics that may carry clinical significance.
No clinical validation: the system has not been validated against real patient outcomes or reviewed by clinical professionals. Results should not be applied in any diagnostic context.

Future directions include integration of continuous biomarker features, deep learning approaches for higher-dimensional input spaces, and prospective validation against de-identified clinical records under appropriate ethical review.
